Why position changes your range

A range is every hand you would play the same way in a spot. The later you sit, the more hands belong in your opening range. Three forces push it wider as you move toward the button.

Fewer players left to act

Every player behind you is another chance someone holds a hand that crushes yours. At a 6-max table, first position has five players who can wake up with a big hand; the button has only two.

Position after the flop

Acting last lets you see what opponents do before you decide. The button is in position against everyone, which turns marginal hands into profitable ones. The blinds are always out of position.

The blinds are there to win

Opening risks 2.5bb to win the 1.5bb already in the pot. That works if everyone folds about 62% of the time. With fewer players to get through, late seats hit that mark far more often.

Patterns to notice

Pairs and suited aces open from every seat. Pairs can flop a set, and suited aces make the nut flush and block the opponent's aces.

Suited hands widen before offsuit ones. Being suited adds equity and makes the hand easier to play after the flop, so K5s opens from the cutoff while K5o never does.

Offsuit hands with weak kickers are the last to join. Hands like A4o and K8o only open from the button and small blind, where fewer players can punish the weak kicker.

Defending follows the same logic

When someone opens before you, two things set how wide you continue: how strong their range is, and your own seat. Wider openers get defended against more; seats with position or a discount defend more.

The math behind the trainer

Every number the trainer shows comes from a handful of ideas: equity, ranges, pot odds, realization and expected value. This page explains each one, shows the exact formula the trainer uses, and lets you try the calculations yourself.

1. Raw equity

Raw equity is your share of the pot if all the remaining cards were dealt out right now and nobody bet again. It is a pure card-strength number: how often your hand ends up best at showdown, with ties counted as half a win.

Against one specific hand it is an exact matchup. For example, A♠K♠ against Q♥Q♦ before the flop is about 46% to 54%. Against a range (every hand an opponent could have) it is the average over all those hands, weighted by how likely each one is.

Equity = (wins + ½ × ties) ÷ runouts dealt

The trainer estimates it by simulation. It deals out the rest of the board and an opponent hand from their range thousands of times, then counts how often you win. That is why numbers can move by a point or two between identical spots.

Raw equity ignores betting. It assumes you always see the river for free, which is exactly why the trainer adjusts it into realized equity (section 5).

2. Combos and card removal

There are 1,326 possible two-card starting hands. They group into 169 hand types:

Hand typeCombosWhy
Pocket pair (e.g. QQ)64 suits choose 2
Suited (e.g. AKs)4one per suit
Offsuit (e.g. AKo)124 × 3 suit pairs

Ranges are weighed in combos, not hand types. That is why offsuit hands make up most of a wide range, and why a percentage like "the button opens 44%" means 44% of 1,326 combos.

Card removal: every card you hold or see on the board removes combos from your opponent's range. Holding the A♠ leaves only 3 combos of AA instead of 6. Cards that remove an opponent's strong hands are called blockers, and they are one reason some hands make better bluffs than others.

3. Ranges and how they narrow

A range starts as every hand a player would play that way preflop. Each action afterwards reshapes it. The trainer gives every combo a weight and multiplies it by how likely that hand is to take the action you saw.

new weight = old weight × P(this hand takes this action)

Example: an opponent checks the flop. Their sets bet 80% of the time, so a set keeps 20% of its weight. Their air bets 30% of the time, so it keeps 70%. After the check, their range holds proportionally more weak hands. This is Bayes' rule applied hand by hand, and it is why the range grids in the Postflop lab fade some hands and not others.

Value

Top pair with a good kicker or better: overpairs, two pair, sets, straights, flushes.

Medium

Top pair with a weak kicker, middle and bottom pair, underpairs. Has showdown value but rarely wants a big pot.

Draws

Flush draws and open-ended straight draws without a strong pair. Gains value on later cards. Counts as air on the river.

Air

No pair and no strong draw. Wins mainly when the opponent folds.

Range advantage compares the share of value hands in each range. Nut advantage compares the share of two pair or better. The side with more strong hands can bet more often and bigger.

4. Pot odds and required equity

When someone bets, the price of calling tells you how much equity you need to break even.

Required equity = call ÷ (pot + bet + call)

Facing a half-pot bet into 10bb: you call 5 to win a final pot of 10 + 5 + 5 = 20, so you need 5 ÷ 20 = 25%.

Minimum defense frequency = pot ÷ (pot + bet)

If you fold more often than this, any two cards profit as a bluff against you. It applies heads up.

Bluff break-even = bet ÷ (pot + bet)

The share of the time a pure bluff must work. A half-pot bluff needs 5 ÷ 15 = 33% folds.

Outs: cards that improve you to the likely best hand. With one card to come, equity ≈ outs × 2%; with two cards to come and no more betting, ≈ outs × 4%. A flush draw has 9 outs, an open-ended straight draw 8, a gutshot 4.

5. Realized equity

Raw equity assumes free cards to the river. In real hands you sometimes fold the best hand to later bets, and sometimes win pots you were behind in by betting. Realized equity is the share of the pot you actually capture once betting is included.

Realized equity = raw equity × realization factor (R)
SituationEffect on RWhy
In position×1.00You act last, see their action first and control the pot.
Out of position×0.85 on the flop, ×0.90 on the turnYou face bets without knowing what comes next.
RiverR = 1No cards left, so raw and realized equity match.
Multiway pot×0.90More players means more chances someone bets you off your hand.
Player still to act behind you×0.90A call can still be raised.
Strong draw+0.05Implied odds: you win extra when you hit.
Weak pair / no pair, no draw−0.05 / −0.10Hard to continue against later bets.

These are simplified rules of thumb, not solver output. The has a step-by-step visual of where the lost equity goes.

6. Expected value (EV)

EV is the average number of big blinds a decision wins over many repetitions. The trainer measures every option relative to folding, so folding is always 0 and chips already in the pot count as gone.

EV(call) = R × equity × (pot + 2 × bet) − bet
EV(bet B) = F × pot + (1 − F) × [R × eqcalled × (pot + 2B) − B]

F is how often they fold, and eqcalled is your equity against only the hands that call. Betting earns twice: when they fold, and when worse hands call.

EV(check, in position) = R × equity × pot

Out of position, checking also accounts for the opponent betting behind you, and you then calling or folding, whichever is better.

EV(raise to X) = F × (pot + bet) + (1 − F) × [R × eqcalled × final pot − your extra chips]

EV given up on any decision is the EV of the best option minus the EV of the option you chose.

6b. How the trainer computes EV, step by step

1. Their range

Start from the opponent's preflop chart range for their seat and action, then reweight every combo by how likely it is to have taken each action since (Bayes' rule, section 3).

2. Your equity

Simulate the rest of the board and their hand a few hundred times (section 1), against the whole range or just the part that continues.

3. Their response

For each option, estimate how often they fold, call or raise. This is where the model choice matters: exploitative or balanced (below).

4. Realization and EV

Multiply equity by the realization factor (section 5) and plug everything into the formulas above. The trainer looks one street ahead; later streets are folded into realization.

Exploitative EV assumes opponents keep playing exactly like the trainer's bots, leaks included. Against a home player who folds too much to big bets, it says bluff big. That is maximum profit if the read is right.

Balanced (GTO-like) EV assumes opponents defend exactly their minimum defense frequency with their strongest hands, and bet with value and bluffs in balanced proportions. It cannot be exploited, but it leaves money on the table against weak players.

Real winners mix the two: start balanced, then lean exploitative as your read on a player firms up. The Play tab's Strategy mix slider sets that weight.

Blended EV = w × exploitative EV + (1 − w) × balanced EV

When to lean which way

OpponentSuggested mix

7. Bet sizing and stack depth

Value bets

Choose the largest size that worse hands still call. Against players who call too much, that size is bigger, sometimes an overbet.

Bluffs

Choose the size with the best folds per chip risked. Bigger bets fold more but must work more often: a pot-size bluff needs 50% folds.

Polarization

Big bets usually mean very strong hands or bluffs. Small bets come from wide ranges, which is why they give you a great price.

Stack-to-pot ratio

SPR = remaining stack ÷ pot. Below about 3, strong one-pair hands can plan to get all-in. Above 8, big pots need strong hands.

8. How the trainer scores you

EV given up

Best option's EV minus your option's EV, in big blinds. Under 0.15bb counts as a good play, since the model is not that precise.

Efficiency

1 − (EV given up ÷ best possible EV). The share of the available value you actually kept, shown in the Running EV panels.

Session grade

Score = 100 × e−x/12, where x is bb given up per 10 decisions. About 2bb per 10 scores 85 (A−); 6bb scores 61 (C).

Preflop drill

Graded against the charts. EV comes from simulating the players behind you continuing with their chart ranges.

9. More showdown value without a higher VPIP

A winning 6-max VPIP is usually around 22–27%. Raising your win rate comes from realizing more equity with the hands you already play, not from playing more hands.

Check back medium hands in position

Middle pair or weak top pair wants to reach showdown cheaply. Betting folds out worse hands and gets called by better ones. Checking keeps bluffs in and controls the pot size.

Call more, raise less, with showdown value

Raising a medium hand turns it into a bluff. Raise for value with strong hands, or as a semi-bluff with draws that can improve. Call with hands in between.

Swap hands, don't add them

Replace offsuit flats like KTo and QJo with suited hands like K9s and 76s. Your VPIP stays the same, but the new hands make more flushes and straights and realize better.

Be the one in position

Flat more from the button and less from the blinds and early seats. Out of position, prefer 3-bet or fold so you start the flop with the initiative.

Tighten up multiway

With more opponents, someone often has a strong hand. Top pair loses value, bluffs work less often, and draws to the nuts gain value. Bet big hands and nut draws, and check most everything else.

Bet with a reason

Bet when worse hands call, when better hands fold, or to deny equity to overcards and draws. If a bet does none of these, check and take your showdown value.

10. What the model does not do

The trainer is a teaching model, not a solver. EV looks one street ahead and folds later streets into the realization factor. Opponents follow fixed strategies by hand strength rather than balanced solver strategies, and preflop charts are simplified to one action per hand. Treat the numbers as reliable about direction (which option is better and roughly by how much) rather than exact to the decimal.

2. Draws: call, semi-bluff, or are you committed?

With a draw you have two ways to win: hitting your outs, and fold equity when you bet or raise. How committed you are depends on how much of your stack is already in compared with what is left.

3. Positions and ranges set up your bluffs

Position

In position you see their action first, so you bluff after they show weakness and can give up cheaply. Out of position, bluffs need more fold equity because you face a decision on every later street. Check-raise bluffs work best with strong draws behind them.

Range advantage

The preflop raiser holds more big pairs and big aces, so high boards like A-K-7 favor them: bet often and small. The big blind holds more small connected hands, so low boards like 7-6-5 favor the caller: the raiser should bluff less there.

Capped ranges

A player who only called preflop rarely has AA or KK, and a player who checks back a street rarely has the nuts. Those ranges are capped, which makes big bets and overbets against them powerful.

Seats and range width

Wider ranges hold more air. The button and blinds defend wide, so their ranges miss more boards. An early-position opener or a cold-caller has a tight, strong range: bluff them less.

Telling a consistent story

Your line should look like the value hands you could have. Betting flop and turn, then shoving a river that completes a draw you could hold, is believable. A sudden river bet after checking twice usually is not.

Blockers are the tiebreaker

On the flop and turn, choose bluffs by equity first: draws that can improve. On the river, with no cards to come, blockers decide which no-equity hands bluff: hold cards that remove their calls and leave their folds.

Read ranges

Follow one opponent through a hand. At every decision, see how their whole range splits between actions, and practice predicting it before it is revealed.

How to put a player on a range

1. Seat and action

An early open is tight and strong; a button open or a big-blind call is wide. Start from that preflop range.

2. The player

Nits play far fewer hands, stations and maniacs far more. Shift the range to match what you know about them.

3. Each action filters

Bets come from value and bluffs, calls from medium hands and draws, checks from weaker hands and traps. Keep only hands that would act this way.

4. Board and size

Ask which hands connect with each card, and remember big bets and raises are more polarized than small ones.
